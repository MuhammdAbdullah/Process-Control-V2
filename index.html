Worksheet 2 — Open-Loop Step Response and Process Dynamics

WS 2
‹ PrevNext ›

Introduction

Before you can design any controller you must have a mathematical model of the process. This worksheet uses the open-loop step-response method — the most practical way to identify a temperature system. With the heater at 0% power and the temperature fully stable, you will step the power directly to 25% and record how the temperature climbs from its old steady state to a new one. Three numbers describe this climb entirely: the process gain Kproc (how many °C of temperature rise you get per percent of power), the dead time L (the seconds that pass before the temperature starts to move at all), and the time constant τ (how fast the temperature rises once it does start moving — specifically the time to reach 63.2% of the total rise). You will repeat the test with the fan running at 50% to see how cooling load changes the model.

T63.2 = Tstart + 0.632 × (Tfinal − Tstart)
τ = t63.2 − L

L = dead time (delay before temperature begins to rise). τ = time constant (speed of response).

So What?

K_proc, L and τ are not abstract numbers — they are the recipe for every tuning rule in WS 8 and WS 9. If your model is wrong, every controller calculated from it will be mis-tuned. Measuring these values accurately here pays dividends in every subsequent worksheet.

Key Takeaways

  • Process gain Kproc = ΔPV ÷ ΔMV: a bigger number means the process is more sensitive to heater power changes.
  • Dead time L is the delay before temperature begins to change after a step input — it cannot be controlled away.
  • Time constant τ describes speed of response; a large τ means the process is slow to reach its new steady state.
  • Fan cooling reduces Kproc (less temperature rise per % power) and usually reduces τ (faster heat removal).
  • Both L and τ are needed for Ziegler-Nichols reaction-curve tuning (WS 9).

Worksheet 3 — ON/OFF Control and Hysteresis

WS 3
‹ PrevNext ›

Introduction

ON/OFF control is the simplest form of automatic feedback: the heater is driven fully ON when temperature falls below (SP − Hysteresis/2) and fully OFF when it rises above (SP + Hysteresis/2). This creates a characteristic oscillation around the setpoint called ripple. The width of the dead-band (hysteresis) controls the trade-off between accuracy and switching frequency. A 1°C hysteresis gives tight temperature control but the heater switches on and off very rapidly — a problem for relays and heater elements with limited mechanical life. A 10°C hysteresis switches slowly but allows large temperature swings. You will measure this trade-off directly across three hysteresis settings and then observe how the fan disturbance shifts the duty cycle.

What You Need to Know

Ripple = Tmaximum − Tminimum

ON/OFF control drives the heater either fully ON or fully OFF. Hysteresis creates a switching band — small hysteresis gives tighter control but causes more switching.

So What?

But it is fundamentally unable to hold a steady temperature: the output is always either 100% or 0%, so the temperature always overshoots in both directions. Understanding its limitations is what motivates proportional, integral, and derivative control in the next worksheets.

Key Takeaways

  • ON/OFF control produces inherent oscillation (ripple) around the setpoint — it cannot achieve a perfectly steady temperature.
  • Smaller hysteresis → smaller ripple but higher switching frequency → faster relay wear.
  • Larger hysteresis → lower switching frequency but larger temperature swings.
  • The fan disturbance shifts the duty cycle: the heater spends more time ON to compensate for extra cooling.
  • Cycle time and ripple together characterise ON/OFF performance — record both.

Worksheet 4 — Proportional Control and Offset

WS 4
‹ PrevNext ›

Introduction

Proportional control replaces the ON/OFF bang with a continuous heater output that is directly proportional to the error (SP − PV). When the temperature is far below target, the heater runs hard; as temperature approaches the setpoint, the heater output reduces proportionally. This eliminates the constant cycling of ON/OFF control, giving a smooth, stable response. However, P-only control almost always settles at a temperature slightly below the setpoint — this residual difference is called steady-state offset. The baseline gain Kp,base = 1/Kproc gives the heater output needed to drive 1°C of error to 100%. You will test gains below, at, and above this baseline, observe the offset at each, and then disturb the process with the fan to see how P-only copes.

What You Need to Know

Output = Kp × (SP − PV)
Kp,base = 1 / Kproc
Offset = SP − PVstable

So What?

Proportional control is the foundation of all PID controllers — P without I or D is the starting point for every tuning exercise. Its fundamental limitation — steady-state offset — directly motivates integral action (WS 5). The higher you push K_p to reduce offset, the closer you get to instability (oscillation). This tension between performance and stability is the central challenge of control engineering.

Key Takeaways

  • P control output = Kp × error; the output is proportional to — and only exists because of — current error.
  • Steady-state offset is an inherent property of P-only control: to produce any output the error must be non-zero.
  • Higher Kp reduces offset but can cause oscillation or instability.
  • Kp,base = 1/Kproc is a good starting point — it produces 100% output for 1°C of error.
  • A fan disturbance increases the offset because more heater output is needed and P-only cannot generate it without a larger error.

Worksheet 5 — PI Control and Disturbance Rejection

WS 5
‹ PrevNext ›

Introduction

Integral action solves the offset problem by accumulating error over time. Even a tiny persistent error is integrated (summed) until the controller has produced enough output to drive that error to zero. The integral term Ki∫e dt keeps growing as long as any error exists — it only stops when PV = SP. Adding integral to proportional gives a PI controller, the most widely used controller type in industry. The cost of integral action is a tendency to overshoot the setpoint (the integral has "wound up" too much before the error reverses) and slower disturbance rejection if I is too large. You will compare P-only against PI at a small and larger I value, then apply the fan disturbance to test how quickly each controller recovers.

What You Need to Know

Output = Kpe + Ki∫e dt

P reacts to the present error. I accumulates errors over time and removes steady-state offset. Too much I causes overshoot and hunting.

So What?

PI control eliminates steady-state offset — the single most important improvement over P-only. That is why roughly 90% of industrial control loops use PI or PID rather than P alone. The integral term means the controller "remembers" past errors and will not rest until they are gone. The worksheet also introduces the concept of integral windup in a disturbance context — a preview of the dedicated anti-windup worksheet (WS 10).

Key Takeaways

  • Integral action eliminates steady-state offset by accumulating error until PV = SP.
  • PI output = Kpe + Ki∫e dt: both terms act simultaneously.
  • Too much integral (large Ki) causes overshoot and slow, oscillatory settling.
  • Too little integral leaves a small residual offset or very slow correction.
  • After a fan disturbance, PI recovers to SP with zero offset; P-only does not.

Worksheet 6 — PID Control, Derivative Action and Frequency

WS 6
‹ PrevNext ›

Introduction

The derivative term adds a third dimension to the controller: it acts on the rate of change of error. If temperature is moving toward the setpoint very rapidly, the derivative term reduces the heater output early — preventing the overshoot that would otherwise result from integrator momentum. This gives PID its characteristic fast, well-damped response. The controller update frequency (how many times per second the PID calculation runs) also matters: a very low frequency means the controller reacts sluggishly; a very high frequency amplifies measurement noise through the derivative term. You will test the same 40→60°C step with PI and then PID at three different update frequencies (0.2, 0.7, and 1.2 Hz) and compare rise time, overshoot and settling behaviour.

What You Need to Know

Output = Kpe + Ki∫e dt + Kd(de/dt)
Ts = 1 / f

P reacts to present error. I accumulates error. D reacts to rate of change. Frequency determines how often the PID calculation updates.

So What?

Derivative action is optional — many loops work well with just PI — but for fast processes or where overshoot is costly (pharmaceutical reactors, food pasteurisers, precision ovens) it is invaluable. The frequency experiment shows that the digital sampling rate is a real design constraint: too slow and you lose control quality; too fast and derivative noise dominates. Real industrial controllers offer derivative filtering for exactly this reason.

Key Takeaways

  • Derivative term = Kd × (de/dt): it predicts where the error is heading and acts early.
  • Derivative reduces overshoot and improves settling for fast step changes.
  • Too much derivative amplifies sensor noise, causing erratic output.
  • Controller update frequency Ts = 1/f: lower frequency means slower response and more overshoot.
  • A good rule of thumb: sample at least 5–10× faster than the process time constant τ.

Worksheet 8 — Ziegler–Nichols Closed-Loop Tuning

WS 8
‹ PrevNext ›

Introduction

Ziegler-Nichols closed-loop tuning (also called the "ultimate gain" method) is a systematic procedure that determines PID gains directly from the process's stability boundary. You increase Kp (with I and D at zero) until the temperature oscillates continuously at a constant amplitude — this is the ultimate gain Ku. The period of those oscillations is the ultimate period Tu. From Ku and Tu alone, empirical formulas give good starting gains for PI or PID controllers. The method works even without any prior process model, which is why it remains popular in industry nearly 80 years after its publication. You will use measured Tu from three separate oscillation cycles and average them to reduce measurement error.

What You Need to Know

PI formulas
Kp = 0.45 Ku
Ti = 0.83 Tu
Ki = Kp / Ti
PID formulas
Kp = 0.6 Ku
Ti = 0.5 Tu
Td = 0.125 Tu
Ki = Kp/Ti, Kd = KpTd

So What?

Ziegler-Nichols is the "classic" tuning method — you will see it referenced in every controls textbook and most industrial controller manuals. Understanding it gives you a common language with other engineers and a reliable fallback when a process model is unavailable. It tends to produce aggressive (fast but slightly oscillatory) controllers, so the calculated gains are often used as a starting point and then refined manually.

Key Takeaways

  • Ku is the gain at which the closed-loop system sustains steady oscillation — the stability boundary.
  • Tu is the period of those sustained oscillations; average at least three cycles for accuracy.
  • Z-N PI formulas: Kp = 0.45 Ku, Ti = 0.83 Tu, Ki = Kp/Ti.
  • Z-N PID formulas: Kp = 0.6 Ku, Ti = 0.5 Tu, Td = 0.125 Tu.
  • Z-N typically produces ~25% overshoot — acceptable as a starting point, then adjust manually.

Introduction

The reaction-curve method (also called open-loop Ziegler-Nichols) calculates PID gains from the open-loop step-response model you identified in WS 2 — specifically Kproc, dead time L, and time constant τ. Worksheet 10 — Integral Windup, Anti-Windup and Final Comparison

WS 10
‹ Prev

Introduction

Integral windup is a hidden failure mode of PI and PID controllers. When the heater output is physically limited (saturated at 0% or 100%), the controller can no longer affect the process — yet the integral term keeps accumulating error. When the limitation is eventually removed, the integrator has built up a huge value ("wound up") and drives the output to the opposite extreme, causing a large and slow-recovering overshoot. Anti-windup prevents this by pausing or clamping the integrator whenever the output saturates. In this worksheet you will deliberately trigger windup by starting at room temperature and commanding a large step — forcing the heater to saturate at 100% — first with anti-windup disabled, then with it enabled. You will then assemble a complete comparison of all controller types tested across the course.

What You Need to Know

Heater Output is limited: 0% ≤ Output ≤ 100%
When Output reaches 100% the heater cannot provide more power.
If the Integral term keeps accumulating, the controller may stay at 100% after the disturbance is removed — this is integral windup.
Anti-windup limits unnecessary integral accumulation.

So What?

Integral windup is one of the most common causes of poor control performance in real plants — especially during startup, after a trip, or following a large setpoint change. Anti-windup is now standard in every serious PID implementation, yet it is often left at default settings that are not ideal for the process. Understanding when and why windup occurs allows you to diagnose mysterious overshoot problems that appear only under specific operating conditions.

Key Takeaways

  • Integral windup occurs when the output is saturated and the integrator continues accumulating error.
  • Without anti-windup: large overshoot and slow recovery after saturation is released.
  • With anti-windup: the integrator is clamped during saturation; recovery is fast and controlled.
  • The effect is most visible during large setpoint steps or start-from-cold conditions.
